01 / 04

County Budget Execution & Variance Analysis

Economic principle

Public finance separates an approved budget from an executed one. The gap between them, budget absorption, matters because unspent capital budgets represent public investment that never happened: roads not built, clinics not equipped. Kenya's Public Finance Management Act sets two guardrails on that gap — county governments must direct at least 30% of their budget to development spending, and cap the wage bill at 35% of revenue — precisely because recurrent costs (salaries) tend to crowd out capital spending when budgets come under strain.

The study

The project builds a county budget (approved vs. actual, by department and by economic category — Personnel, Operations, Development), loads it into SQLite, and answers four questions in SQL: absorption by department, absorption by category, whether the two PFM Act rules pass or fail, and which line items under-spent by more than 25%. Python then produces a bar chart of absorption by department, and an Excel report where variance and absorption are live formulas with conditional formatting — the same tool a budget officer would actually use.

Conclusion

On the illustrative data, personnel lines absorbed almost their full allocation (about 98%) while development absorbed only about half — and every line flagged as under-spent by more than 25% was a development line. That is the standard signature of weak execution capacity: money is committed on paper but stalls at procurement, disbursement or contractor delays.

Kenya & African markets context

This mirrors what Kenya's Controller of Budget reports on actual counties year after year — development absorption in the 40–60% range is common, well short of the legal floor. The same pattern shows up across fiscally decentralized African states — Nigeria's states and South Africa's municipalities face comparable capital-absorption gaps — which is why budget execution monitoring, not just budget approval, is treated as a core public financial management discipline by the World Bank and IMF's PEFA framework.

02 / 04

Treasury Bond Pricing & the Yield Curve

Economic principle

A bond's price is the present value of its future coupons and principal, discounted at the market yield. As yields rise, prices fall — but not all bonds fall by the same amount. Duration measures that sensitivity, and the yield curve — yields plotted against maturity — reflects what markets expect about future growth, inflation and risk. An upward-sloping curve is the normal state, compensating investors for locking up money longer; a flat or inverted curve is a signal markets watch closely.

The study

The project prices a semi-annual coupon bond by discounting every cash flow individually, then derives Macaulay duration, modified duration, and convexity from first principles in NumPy — no finance library shortcuts. It shocks yields by ±100 basis points, compares the full repriced value against the duration-plus-convexity approximation, and builds an Excel bond calculator where every result is a formula, not a static number, so changing the coupon or yield recalculates the whole workbook live.

Conclusion

A 100bp rise in yields cuts a 2-year bond's price by about 1.7%, but a 25-year bond's price by about 5.9% — the same rate move, a very different loss, purely a function of maturity. The duration-convexity approximation tracks the true repriced value to within a few hundredths of a shilling, confirming why traders use it as a fast risk proxy instead of repricing every bond from scratch.

Kenya & African markets context

The Central Bank of Kenya's Treasury bond auctions are the benchmark for pricing across the entire Kenyan capital market, from corporate bonds to bank lending rates. A deepening local-currency bond market is also a policy goal shared across African markets — Nigeria, Ghana and Kenya have all pushed to grow domestic debt markets specifically to reduce reliance on foreign-currency borrowing and the exchange-rate risk that comes with it (the same risk modelled directly in Project 4).

03 / 04

NSE Equity Risk, CAPM & Market Surveillance

Economic principle

The Capital Asset Pricing Model says a stock's required return equals the risk-free rate plus its beta — its sensitivity to market-wide moves — times the market risk premium. Only systematic risk, the risk you can't diversify away, should be rewarded. Separately, market integrity depends on regulators being able to distinguish ordinary volatility from abnormal, potentially manipulative trading — the core job of a capital markets surveillance function.

The study

Daily returns and CAPM beta are computed entirely in SQL, using window functions (LAG() for returns, rolling 20-day windows for the surveillance screen) rather than pulling raw prices into Python and doing the math there. Python then annualises risk and return, computes each stock's Sharpe ratio and CAPM alpha, and plots the Security Market Line. A surveillance rule flags any day where a stock's price move exceeds three standard deviations and volume exceeds three times its trailing average — using only past data, so the rule never has hindsight it wouldn't have had in real time.

Conclusion

Higher-beta stocks carried more risk and, on the simulated data, more return — consistent with CAPM's basic prediction. Out of two years of daily trading across five stocks, the surveillance screen caught exactly one event: a single planted anomaly, an 11% move on roughly six times normal volume. It did not fire on any ordinary trading day, which is the behaviour a regulator wants from a screen — high precision, not noise.

Kenya & African markets context

The Nairobi Securities Exchange, like most African exchanges outside the JSE in South Africa, trades with thinner liquidity than developed markets, which makes abnormal-volume screens especially useful — a large move on low volume can be noise, but a large move on unusually high volume is exactly what a compliance officer at the CMA would want surfaced for review. A flag is a prompt to investigate, never proof of wrongdoing on its own.

04 / 04

Public Debt Sustainability: a Monte Carlo Simulation

Economic principle

Debt sustainability comes down to one comparison: the interest rate a government pays on its debt (i) versus the growth rate of its economy (g). When i exceeds g, debt grows on its own — the "snowball" effect — and the government needs primary budget surpluses just to hold the debt ratio steady. For a country like Kenya that borrows partly in foreign currency, a weaker shilling directly raises the local-currency value of that debt, adding a second channel of risk on top of interest rates and growth.

The study

The project implements the standard debt-dynamics identity, d[t] = d[t-1](1+i)/(1+g) − pb, with a blended interest rate that accounts for the foreign-currency share of debt and shilling depreciation. Because growth, inflation, interest rates, the exchange rate and the primary balance are uncertain and correlated with each other, the model draws 10,000 correlated future paths (via a Cholesky decomposition of the shock correlations) over a 10-year horizon, and compares four scenarios: baseline, fiscal consolidation, a currency shock, and higher domestic interest rates.

Conclusion

Under the illustrative baseline assumptions, the primary balance needed to stabilise debt exceeds the assumed one, so the debt ratio drifts upward over the simulation. Of the four scenarios, a sustained shilling depreciation does the most damage — debt ends above today's level in nearly every simulated path — while a 2.5%-of-GDP fiscal consolidation is enough to turn the trajectory around.

Kenya & African markets context

This is the same type of analysis the IMF and World Bank run as a formal Debt Sustainability Analysis for Kenya and other African economies. It's directly relevant to Kenya's own debt trajectory — including its Eurobond exposure and shilling depreciation risk — and to the wider continent, where Zambia's and Ghana's debt distress in recent years were driven by exactly this combination: dollar- denominated debt colliding with currency depreciation and growth shortfalls.
